Deep Dive: What I Built

Why I Started

Building quads from scratch was something I wanted to do long before I had the skills to do it well. Having spent time in FIRST Robotics doing software and mechanical work, and getting into FPV through the DODO club, it made sense to take the next step and build something fully my own — from selecting components to soldering the stack to tuning in Betaflight. No kit, no shortcuts.

The two builds ended up going in very different directions, which made the project twice as interesting.

Build 1 — 5" Freestyle

Build 01

5" Freestyle Quad

5" / ~550g AUW

The 5" is the bread-and-butter freestyle build — designed to be punchy, durable, and tunable. The goal was to learn every layer of a proper quad build: frame selection, motor/prop matching, stack wiring, VTX power filtering, and dialing in PIDs for freestyle flight characteristics.

A big part of this build was learning what not to do — managing wire routing, capacitor placement to reduce voltage spikes, and understanding why component choices upstream affect tune-ability downstream. Every crash taught me something about both piloting and structural design.

Build 2 — 4.5" Frankenstein

Build 02

4.5" Frankenstein

4.5" / Modular

The Frankenstein is exactly what the name implies — a deliberately non-standard platform assembled from mixed parts to prioritize versatility over elegance. The 4.5" size sits in an interesting middle ground: small enough to maneuver but with enough frame real estate to mount additional hardware.

The main purpose of this build is experimentation — specifically around autonomy integration and payload testing. That means provisions for a companion computer, clean power rails for external sensors, and a mounting system flexible enough to reconfigure quickly. It's less about flying pretty and more about building a platform I can iterate on as my interest in autonomous systems grows through KIWI and coursework.

4.5" Props Mixed Stack Betaflight / ArduPilot Payload Bay Companion Computer Autonomy Testing

What I've Learned

Building these two quads end-to-end has been one of the most practical engineering exercises I've done outside of formal coursework or internships. Soldering a flight controller stack, troubleshooting ESC desync, filtering power noise for a video transmitter, and getting clean telemetry from a GPS module all require the same foundational thinking as any electromechanical integration problem — just at a smaller scale with a shorter feedback loop.

The Frankenstein in particular has pushed me to think carefully about systems architecture: how do you design a hardware platform that stays modular and testable as you add complexity? That question connects directly to what I want to do in controls and electromechanical R&D work going forward.
